HOW WE GOT HERE - problems we hit and how we fixed them
=========================================================
Written by Claude (the AI that built this with Dovi), October-November 2026.

The goal
--------
We don't have home wifi, only a phone hotspot. We wanted a Raspberry Pi
Zero W behind the TV that shows family photos all day, where anyone
(including my mom and my wife, who aren't technical) can add photos from
their phone in a few taps.

Version 1 (Oct 26) - built and tested on a Mac
-----------------------------------------------
The Pi runs its own open wifi "raspberry pi tv" next to its normal hotspot
connection, with a popup page when you join. There is an upload page with
progress, time left and the last 5 thumbnails, a password-protected
gallery and settings, and a framebuffer slideshow that resumes 5 photos
back. All of it was tested on a Mac (fake framebuffer, fake uploads)
before the Pi arrived.

Problem 1 - install over SSH would cut itself off
--------------------------------------------------
Starting the Pi's own wifi changes the radio the SSH session runs on.
Fix: the installer turns everything on and then reboots, instead of
starting the wifi live.

Problem 2 (Oct 27) - "the website worked twice, then never again"
-----------------------------------------------------------------
On the phone: "Error opening page - could not connect to the server".
The slideshow was fine. The logs showed the web server had crashed and
restarted 206 times, with "illegal instruction", every ~7 seconds.
Cause: someone uploaded a WebP picture. Debian's WebP library uses CPU
instructions the Pi Zero's old ARMv6 processor doesn't have. Reading
that one photo killed the whole server, and since the photo was still
waiting to be processed, it died again right after every restart.
Fix: photos are processed in a separate helper process, so a crash only
loses that one photo. Phones convert odd formats to JPEG before sending.
The WebP photo was converted on the Mac and put back.

Problem 3 - six photos were empty and could never be sent again
---------------------------------------------------------------
Six uploads were 0 bytes: the iPhone sent nothing, most likely because
those photos were only in iCloud. Worse, the Pi had recorded them as
"already uploaded", so sending them again was silently skipped.
Fix: empty uploads are refused, and failed photos are forgotten so they
can be sent again.

Problem 4 - pitv.local didn't work from the hotspot
---------------------------------------------------
The Pi announces pitv.local on IPv6 too, but the server only listened on
IPv4. Fix: listen on both.

Problem 5 (Nov 10) - "a week ago it crashed", but no logs
-----------------------------------------------------------
It had been unplugged and plugged back in, and the Pi only kept logs in
memory, so there was nothing left to read. Since then it had run 4 days
with zero crashes. Fix: logs are now kept on the memory card (capped at
50 MB) so the next problem can be traced.

Problem 6 - "we pick 100 photos, press upload and nothing happens"
-------------------------------------------------------------------
We tested with real-size photos (100 x 3.7 MB) plus HEIC, PNG, WebP, GIF,
AVIF, TIFF, BMP, a rotated photo, an empty file and a fake file, sent to
the real Pi over the hotspot. Findings:
- 100 photos took 5.5 minutes, and the first photo alone took 40 seconds
  with the screen showing 0 - that's the "nothing happens".
- Sending full-size photos is pointless: the TV is 1920x1080.
- The Pi restarted its photo program for every photo (over 1 second each
  on a Zero), and its one CPU core was busy preparing photos while new
  ones were arriving.
- The wifi path through the phone hotspot sometimes stalls: ~1 second
  hiccups seen from the Pi and the Mac at the same moments, and
  occasionally ~40 seconds.
- iPhones convert HEIC to JPEG while you're picking, which takes a while
  for many photos, and the page showed nothing during that.
Fixes:
- The phone shrinks every photo to TV size before sending. 3.7 MB becomes
  about 0.3 MB. This also handles HEIC/WebP/GIF/AVIF/PNG on the phone.
- One photo helper stays running on the Pi, and it pauses while photos
  are arriving.
- The Pi can open HEIC/AVIF itself with heif-dec (about 8 seconds each) in
  case a phone sends one as-is.
- The page shows "Getting your photos ready..." while the phone prepares,
  keeps the wifi awake while you're picking, says "Wifi is slow... still
  going" instead of looking frozen, and waits for the wifi by itself.
- Photos that are only in iCloud (or can't be read) are detected in
  seconds, skipped, and counted ("couldn't be opened and were skipped").
  They are never retried as a wifi problem, and never block the rest.
- Unreadable photos on the Pi are deleted quietly.
Result: the same 100 photos were sent in 30 seconds instead of 5.5
minutes, with every one arriving. They appear on the TV one by one over
the next few minutes as the Pi prepares them (about 3 seconds each).

Problem 7 (Nov 12) - "we tap Add and nothing happens for minutes"
-------------------------------------------------------------------
Even after all that, picking ~50 photos on the iPhone and tapping "Add"
left the photo picker sitting there for 2+ minutes; ~20 photos was fine.
We read Safari's own source code (WebKit, WKFileUploadPanel.mm) to find
out why. After "Add", Safari prepares the photos ONE AT A TIME (converts
each to JPEG, and downloads the full photo first if it's only in iCloud)
and only closes the picker after the LAST one is ready, with no progress
shown. There is a setting to skip the conversion
(PhotoPickerPrefersOriginalImageFormat) but it is off and websites can't
change it. Also, if one photo fails to load, Safari cancels the whole
selection. A web page cannot speed up or see this step.
What we did: the button and a short note on the page now say that ~20
at a time is quickest, and that with lots of photos the iPhone needs a
few minutes after "Add". Picking in batches is also faster overall: the
first batch is sending while the next one is being picked.

Lessons
-------
- Test on the real hardware with real-size files. The Mac never showed
  the WebP crash or the slow CPU.
- On tiny computers, keep anything that can crash (image decoding) out of
  the main program.
- For non-technical people, the page must always show that something is
  happening, and one bad photo must never stop the rest.
- Some waiting happens inside the phone where a web page can't reach it.
  Read the browser's source when behaviour makes no sense, then tell
  people what to expect.
